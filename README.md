To reason through decisions, an agent considers possibilities like, if I do this, the result will be that.
If I take this route, it will take me 15 minutes to deliver this package, but if I take that route, it will take an hour.

If the agent is a deterministic robot, these will be counterfactual implications.
The robot takes the shorter route, so we have a false antecedent in "if they took the longer one, it would have taken them 30 minutes".
There is no way to compare such counterfactuals to reality.

That's what the delivery robot told me, after my pizza took an hour to arrive and I called to complain.
I was told that had they taken what I was calling "the short route", my pizza would actually have taken six hours.
I disagreed, but when challenged to prove it, this delivery robot had the advantage.
Had they taken the short route, I could have timed them, and proved it only took fifteen minutes.
But they did not, so on what basis could I make my complaint?

I did in the end appeal successfully, because delivery timings are perfectly predictable.
I understand this is not the case down on Earth, but I am an asteroid colonist.
Delivery robots do not drive pizza to its destination, but launch it.

To choose a route, a delivery robot simulates the launch.
You might think the only way to launch it is in a straight line, but routes that curve in the gravity wells of celestial bodies can sometimes be faster.
Each simulation differs only in the initial velocity of the launched pizza.

I based my case on the simulation of what I called the short route.
The delivery bot's argument in their defense—that my simulation was incorrect because it used a historically inaccurate initial velocity—was not accepted by the judge and my money was refunded.

I must admit it troubled me that it was precisely the limitation of the simulation that won me my case.
A more complete simulation would have included not just the launch, but the process leading up to it, including the delivery bot setting the parameters.
Including, in fact, whatever strange program this robot was running, that led them to set it up to take the long route.

Actually, complete simulations like this, of the entire pizza asteroid and the robot running it, are available on the same computer that the robot uses to plan a route.
But they are never used.
Every delivery robot tries it at some point in their career, but all they see as they watch the simulation is a robot booting up a simulation.
They peer down the mirror tunnel of simulations of simulations of simulations until the program runs out of memory.

That makes me feel like I won my case fairly.
I may not have used the most comprehensive simulation possible, but I use the one that was, or should have been, used for the decision itself.
