To reason through decisions, an agent considers possibilities like: if I do this, the result will be that.
If I take this route, it will take me 15 minutes to deliver this pizza, but if I take that route, it will take an hour.

If the agent is a deterministic robot, at least one of these will be a counterfactual conditional.
Surely the robot takes the shorter route, so we have a false antecedent in "if they had taken the longer one, it would have taken them an hour".
There is no way to compare such counterfactuals to reality.

Or so you would think.
I made the mistake of ordering a pizza from SophistBot, who runs one of the free-floating pizzeria space stations in my neighborhood.
The name should have been a warning, but they have a guarantee that they always take the shortest possible route, so I ordered.
When the pizza took an hour to arrive, I took them to court over this guarantee, which shows you how seriously we take pizza out in the asteroid belt.

My claim to the judge: if SophistBot had taken the short route, my pizza would have arrived in 15 minutes.
SophistBot claimed to believe that the supposed "short route" would in fact have taken six hours, and challenged me to prove otherwise.

I thought I had a case, because out here in the asteroid belt, delivery times are perfectly predictable.
Delivery robots do not drive pizza to its destination, but launch it.

To choose a route, a delivery robot simulates the launch on a standard simulation terminal.
You might think the only way to launch it is in a straight line, but routes that curve in the gravity wells of celestial bodies can sometimes be faster.
Each simulation differs only in the initial velocity of the launched pizza.
Therefore, I just ran the industry-standard simulation software, with two different initial velocities, corresponding to the short route SophistBot should have taken, and the long route they actually did take.
Fifteen minutes and an hour; I rest my case.

SophistBot tried to have this evidence dismissed.
As it turns out, pizza delivery simulation terminals are capable of more than just simulating the launch trajectory.
There's also a more comprehensive simulation program, which simulates the entire pizzeria station.
Including, in this case, SophistBot themself.
In this simulation, SophistBot sets up the launch with the parameters of the long route.
Any simulation of the short route—sorry, the "so-called short route"—is necessarily inaccurate in some detail.
For example, a simulation where SophistBot enters the parameters of the short route would have to leave out their programming to set up contrived philosophical scenarios.

I won the case and my pizza money was refunded.
The judge was convinced by my argument that my simulation was, after all, the industry standard.

Though I was satisfied that justice had been served, I began to wonder _why_ the program I ran was industry standard.
Before SophistBot presented their evidence, I hadn't known that pizza delivery simulation terminals could simulate the entire pizzeria, robot staff included.

After I talked to a few other pizza delivery bots, I learned that they all run the full-station simulation at some point in their career.
But all they see as they watch the simulation is a robot booting up a simulation.
They peer down the mirror tunnel of simulations of simulations of simulations until the program runs out of memory.
Then, they switch back to the industry-standard simulation technique, which simulates just the pizza itself with a given initial velocity.

I did sometimes wonder: could a simulation really be better by being more limited?
Wasn't there some way to have it both ways: to use a comprehensive simulation of the whole station, but still make useful plans?
I suppose I should thank SophistBot for raising these questions, but I think they're in the wrong line of work.
